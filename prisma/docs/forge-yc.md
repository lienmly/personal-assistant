# The road to YC

> The goal for Forge is **getting into Y Combinator**, with no deadline: applications are
> rolling, there are four batches a year, and the plan is to apply to every one of them and
> make each application better than the last until it lands.
>
> The pitch itself is in [the startup brief](./forge-vision.md). This doc is the plan.

---

## The goal, stated so it can't drift

**Apply every batch. Improve between each one. Stop when accepted.**

That framing is genuinely stronger than a deadline would be, and it is worth defending
because it looks lazy from the outside. Four batches a year means a rejection costs about
three months, not a year — which turns the application from a single high-stakes swing into
a quarterly checkpoint that forces the question *what is different since last time?* to be
answered out loud.

It also means **there are no invented due dates on this project**, per CLAUDE.md §6,
*"A goal with no deadline gets no due dates"*. Forty rows with made-up dates on a `side`
project would put an Overdue tile in front of you every morning that could never be right.
What supplies the ordering instead is the track order and a handful of decision rows sitting
in front of the work they gate.

The **one dated row is the batch deadline itself**, because that is a clock somebody else is
holding — and it is dated as a *verification* task rather than as the deadline, because the
current batch structure and dates could not be confirmed from here. See the last section.

## Where this actually stands

Honestly: **Forge is an idea, a written brief, and nothing else.** No name that has been
checked, no domain, no prototype, no users spoken to, no site. That is not a problem — it is
the accurate starting position, and the reason the backlog reads the way it does.

What a YC application is assessed on, roughly, and where each one stands:

- **The founders.** A multilingual mom with real domain expertise in exactly the problem,
  who ships software. This is the strongest part of the application and it is already true.
- **Traction.** Nothing yet. This is what Coding Mom is for, and it is the slowest-moving
  input, which is why it starts first and runs in the background of everything else.
- **A working thing.** Nothing yet. One demo that visibly works beats any amount of deck.
- **Talking to users.** Nothing yet, and it is the cheapest of the four to fix.
- **Clarity.** The brief is broad — AI design studio, $200 credits, a marketplace, and an
  impact lens. That is four companies in a sentence, and the application asks what you are
  making in *one*.

## The order, and why

1. **Coding Mom.** The audience is the slowest input and the only one that cannot be
   sprinted, so it starts first and everything else happens beside it.
2. **Talk to people.** Cheapest, fastest, and it is what stops the next two steps from being
   built on a guess. It also produces the quotes an application is made of.
3. **One prototype that works.** The multilingual book reader, because it is the one being
   built for her anyway, it is the most concrete, and it demos in ten seconds.
4. **The landing page and waitlist.** After there is something to show, because a waitlist
   with nothing behind it collects e-mail addresses that go cold.
5. **Apply.** With whatever exists at that point. The first application's job is to exist and
   to establish a baseline, not to be the one that works.

## The website is two things, and they are months apart

"Build the website" covers both a marketing page and the actual product, and conflating them
is how the small one waits for the big one.

- **The landing page** is a pitch, a waitlist and the flagship products' story. Days of work.
  It is what Coding Mom's link in bio points at, and it is a required piece of the first
  application. It should exist early.
- **The platform** — the AI Design Studio, accounts, the marketplace, manufacturing
  integration — is the actual company and is not a thing that gets finished before an
  application. What it needs for YC is **one slice that visibly works**: describe a thing in
  plain language, get a 3D model back. Even if that slice is mostly a wrapper around an
  existing text-to-CAD model, a demo that does the core magic once is worth more than a
  roadmap that describes it completely.

Deciding what that slice is, and finding out what text-to-CAD can genuinely do today, are
both rows on the board — and the second one may change the pitch. It is the load-bearing
assumption of the whole company and it has not been tested.

## Three things worth knowing before they cost time

**The solo-founder question.** YC funds solo founders and has done for years, but it is on
record as preferring teams, and "why are you solo" is a question the application asks
directly. The answer is much better prepared than improvised — and it is a genuinely good
one here: sole domain expert, already shipping, already building the audience. It is worth
deciding in advance whether the answer is "solo on purpose" or "looking for a technical or
hardware co-founder", because those are different applications and the second one has a
recruiting task hiding behind it.

**The $200 prototype credit is an unpriced claim.** It appears in the tagline, the solution
and the business model, and nobody has asked a contract manufacturer what $200 actually buys
for a one-off run. If the honest number is $600, the pitch changes — better to find that out
from a supplier now than from an investor later. Two conversations fix it.

**The breathing tracker is a regulatory question, not just a build.** Anything that measures
a baby's breathing and implies it will alert you is in territory where the claims are
regulated — a well-funded competitor in this exact space spent years dealing with it. The
product can still be built and shown; what needs checking early is what may be *claimed*
about it, because the claim is the marketing and the marketing is the video. This is the
classic thing that gets found late.

## What the first application will honestly say

Worth drafting cold, long before it is due, because the gaps in the draft are the backlog.

- **What are you making?** One sentence. Not four. The brief currently needs a decision about
  whether the first customer is a *creator* who wants to build and sell, or a *parent* who
  wants to buy the finished thing — the platform serves both eventually, and the sentence can
  only serve one now.
- **Why you?** The strongest section. Write it first; it is free.
- **What have you built?** Whatever the prototype is at that point, plus the audience, plus
  the interviews. This is the section that improves between batches, which is the whole
  strategy.
- **Who wants this and how do you know?** This is what the interviews are for. Numbers and
  quotes, not conviction.

Then, after each rejection: **write down what changed before the next one.** That row exists
on the board on purpose. A quarterly cycle only compounds if the improvement is deliberate,
and "apply again in three months" without it is the same application four times a year.

## The batch dates are unverified

The plan assumes four batches a year on a rolling application, which is what was described
and matches how YC has been running recently — but **no specific deadline has been confirmed
from here, and none is asserted as a due date anywhere on this project.** That follows
CLAUDE.md §6, *"An unverified date is a note, not a due date"*: a wrong deadline on the one
row that has a real external clock is worse than no deadline at all.

The first row in the `YC` track is to check the current batch schedule and the next
application deadline at the source, and it is the only dated task on Forge. Once it is
confirmed, the deadline is real and worth putting on the calendar as an **Event** — the same
treatment Sleepy Cat's Next Fest week got, and for the same reason: it is a thing that
happens at a time rather than a thing you owe.
